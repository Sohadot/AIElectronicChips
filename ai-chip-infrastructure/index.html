Reference layer · 08 Deployment · Evidence release 006

AI Chip Infrastructure

An AI chip becomes useful compute only as part of an operating infrastructure.

Architectures and devices matter, but deployment also requires memory, interconnect, power, cooling, facilities, software, operations, and continuing access. This reference sets out what evidence would show that each of those conditions is met at a site, and how far the published record for one rack-scale system, NVIDIA GB200 NVL72, actually goes.

00

Reference summary

Definition. This page covers Ontology Layer 08: Deployment as the operating infrastructure required to install, run, and sustain AI compute.

Compute sovereignty relationship. A chip is not deployable compute until facilities, power, cooling, operations, and access conditions align in a declared scope.

Boundary. This page does not establish current site readiness, operating guarantees, or entity-level deployment conclusions. Product documents and reference architectures describe what a deployment needs, not what any site has done.

01

Beyond the component

A component-level view can describe technical characteristics. An infrastructure view asks whether the surrounding system can install, energize, connect, operate, maintain, and replace it.

02

Deployment is a dependency layer

Deployment includes facilities, cloud or on-premises access, operational expertise, geographic distribution, maintenance, and recovery options. These are assessed separately rather than assumed.

03

Bounded interpretation

Infrastructure readiness is time-sensitive and entity-specific. A reference model can define the evidence needed, but operational conclusions require current sources and a declared scope.

04

What makes AI compute deployable

An accelerator is deployable compute only when several conditions hold at once, at one site, for one configuration, on one date: the hardware is present, it is energised and cooled, its fabrics are configured, its software and firmware are at a known baseline, the facility can operate and recover it, and the operator may lawfully receive and use it. Each condition has its own evidence, and evidence for one does not carry over to another.

Most of what is published about AI systems describes the first step only: what a product contains and what it needs. NVIDIA's documentation for GB200 NVL72 goes further than most, with installation procedures, bring-up steps and a validation checklist [3]. Even so, these are instructions for a deployment engineer, not records of a deployment. A procedure says what should be checked; only a record from a site says what was found.

This page therefore separates two questions that are often merged. Is the capability documented? can be answered from vendor sources. Is the capability present and working at a site? cannot, and on the evidence reviewed here it remains unanswered for every site.

05

Seven evidence domains

Deployment evidence is organised in seven domains. They are separated because each is documented by different parties and verified by different records, not because they are independent: Section 07 shows where they couple.

  • Compute hardware. The trays, accelerators, switches and enclosures, and the records that they were delivered and installed.
  • Electrical. The supply chain from utility to rack, its capacity and redundancy. Figures and conflicts are on Power and Thermal Limits.
  • Thermal. Liquid and air heat removal and the facility loops behind them, also covered there.
  • Network and interconnect. Scale-up fabrics inside the rack and scale-out and management networks between racks; the fabrics themselves are described on Interconnects.
  • Software. Cluster management, scheduling, drivers and firmware, and the versions that must agree.
  • Facility operations. Building management, leak response, remote access, acceptance and handover.
  • Access and compliance. Export controls and the conditions under which a system may be supplied and used.

07

Hardware and software integration

ANVIDIA states that the software stack for its Grace Blackwell rack systems is composed of software and firmware running on compute nodes, switch nodes and power shelves [1]. Its bring-up procedure advises bringing up the NVLink switches first, so that the NVLink domain is already configured when the GB200 compute trays power on, and asks the engineer to check that firmware levels match those in the software bill of materials [3]. The 2025 administration guide describes Slurm-only scheduling and lists the GPU driver, DCGM, NVSM and DOCA OFED among the base components [4]. The newer rack-setup guide instead documents DGX SuperPOD orchestration through Slurm or Kubernetes (K8s) via RunAI, with racks assigned to their respective workload managers [3]. These are version-scoped vendor statements, not evidence that either configuration has been installed or validated at a site.

CThree consequences follow for evidence. First, a firmware baseline spans electrical equipment: the power shelves run firmware that belongs to the same bill of materials as the GPUs. Second, the advised bring-up order makes the scale-up network a precondition for compute, so a compute record without a fabric record is incomplete. Third, an installed software stack shows that components are present, not that the cluster performs; that would need recorded results of the validation tests NVIDIA names, with configuration and thresholds [4].

UThe versions that must agree are not in the reviewed documents. The rack setup guide refers firmware updates to a standalone NVIDIA DGX GB200/GB300 Firmware Update Guide, which was not reviewed, and the bill of materials is supplied per customer [3].

08

Electrical and cooling evidence

The rack's power and cooling figures, the arithmetic behind them and their conflicts are set out on Power and Thermal Limits and are not repeated here. For deployment evidence, five of them matter because a site cannot be shown to meet requirements that are not defined:

  • URedundancy. One NVIDIA document says the power shelves provide N+N redundancy; another says they are configured as N [1][2].
  • UBus bar. A 1,400 A bus bar [6] at about 50 V carries about 70 kW, against an approximately 120 kW rack; no source states the number of paths.
  • UThe 120 kW figure. Not defined as rated, typical or measured, and not separated between GB200 and GB300.
  • UThe scalable unit. The 1.2 MW design figure for eight racks is not itemised.
  • UCoolant. No coolant type, flow, supply temperature, pressure or liquid-cooling class is stated.

ANVIDIA's deployment checklist contains the items "Power infrastructure correctly configured (sufficient power capacity)", "Cooling infrastructure adequate for GB200/GB300 NVL72 systems" and "Thermal management validated under load" [3]. CThe checklist names these items without thresholds, so a ticked box would record an engineer's judgement against undefined criteria. Electrical and cooling evidence for a site would be its utility agreement, single-line diagram, commissioning results and a recorded thermal test under stated load, none of which was reviewed.

ANVIDIA's power-off procedure turns off all power shelves at AC and waits three to five minutes for the PSU capacitors to discharge [1]. It is a vendor procedure, not a site electrical-safety assessment.

09

Network, operations and access evidence

ANetwork. NVIDIA's north-south networking guide asks for the SN5600 and SN2201 switches to be physically installed before they are deployed [5]. Its administration guide describes a check_nvlink_health test that confirms links are active for each GPU, at the correct speed, with fabric registration complete [4]. The output of such a test for a named rack would be integration evidence; the test's description is not.

ATested scale. The SuperPOD reference architecture states that "A fully tested system scales to two SUs", then, in a visibly garbled line, that a fully tested system "scales for a single SU" [2]. UThis release records the conflict and does not choose between them.

AOperations. NVIDIA describes building-management integration that coordinates power and cooling events, including rapid leakage detection [1]; its administration guide says leak events are collated in Base Command Manager [4]. Its pre-deployment checklist says most Mission Control sites are air-gapped, so every software, firmware and licence artifact is prepared in advance [3]. CLeak response therefore crosses rack, cluster software and facility systems, and evidence for one part does not show that the chain works. On an air-gapped site, the bill of materials becomes part of operating evidence, since every later update follows the same path.

AAccess. On 2024-12-02 the US Bureau of Industry and Security announced new controls on high-bandwidth memory, red-flag guidance on diversion, and a clarification that controls apply to software keys that allow access to specific hardware or software [7]. CExport controls attach to items, destinations, end uses and parties, so evidence here is a classification or licence determination for a named transaction under the current rules. AThe current eCFR text of two sections was also retrieved on 2026-11-11. 15 CFR 742.6(a)(6)(i)(B) states that "A license is required for items specified in ECCNs 3A090.c, 3D001 (for 3A090.c), and 3E001 (for 3A090.c)" when exported, reexported or transferred to or within Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination; its latest amendment in the eCFR history is dated 2026-07-23 [8]. 15 CFR 740.25 sets out a licence exception for HBM, in a single version dated 2024-12-05 [9]. CThese are two sections of the Export Administration Regulations, not the full set of rules that may apply: the ECCN entry itself, end-use and end-user provisions and later amendments were not reviewed. This page gives no legal view and no transaction-level conclusion.

CThe published record for GB200 NVL72 is unusually detailed at the first two stages and empty at the last three. That pattern is expected: installation, commissioning and operating records belong to operators, who rarely publish them. It means the case can show what a deployment requires, but not that one has succeeded.

14

Gaps and unresolved questions

These are material questions the reviewed evidence cannot answer. They are recorded, not filled with general industry commentary.

  1. Site records. No installation, commissioning or operating record for any named GB200 NVL72 site was reviewed.
  2. Acceptance criteria. NVIDIA's checklist names "sufficient power capacity" and cooling "adequate" for the racks without thresholds. [3]
  3. Firmware guide. The standalone GB200/GB300 firmware update guide was not reviewed.
  4. Power and cooling carried from Release 005. Redundancy, bus-bar paths, the basis of 120 kW, the scalable-unit figure and the coolant specification remain open; see Power and Thermal Limits.
  5. Tested scale. Two scalable units or one. [2]
  6. Software versions. The driver, DCGM, DOCA OFED, NVOS and Base Command Manager versions required together.
  7. Current access rules. Beyond the 2024 announcement, only two current sections, 15 CFR 742.6 and 740.25, were reviewed. Other provisions that may apply, and future amendments, were not. [8][9]
  8. GB300 differences. Which deployment requirements differ between GB200 and GB300.
  9. Building-management interface. Signals, protocol and the facility's responsibilities.
