Power and Thermal Limits

Compute capacity is bounded by the ability to deliver power and remove heat.

A chip or system that cannot be reliably energized and cooled is not deployable capacity. This reference follows power from the grid to the accelerator package, and heat from the package back out to the atmosphere. At each stage it records what primary sources establish, what they leave unstated, and how to calculate what follows from the stated figures.

04

Power, energy and heat: terms that are not interchangeable

Power is a rate, measured in watts (W, kW, MW). Energy is power over time, measured in watt-hours (kWh, MWh, TWh). A grid connection, a power shelf and a cooling unit are sized in power. An electricity bill and a national consumption estimate are measured in energy. In steady state, almost all electrical power a system draws leaves it as heat, so the heat to be removed tracks the power actually drawn, not the power the equipment is rated for.

06

The thermal transport chain

Heat leaves the processor junction through the package into a cold plate. A coolant carries it through the rack's technology cooling system (TCS) loop to a coolant distribution unit (CDU). There a heat exchanger passes it to the facility water system (FWS), which rejects it outdoors. ANVIDIA describes this path for direct-to-chip cooling, and OCP defines the TCS and FWS loops. [4][5]

The governing relation. A liquid carries heat according to Q = ṁ × cp × ΔT. Here Q is heat flow in kW, ṁ is mass flow in kg/s, cp is specific heat capacity in kJ/kg·K and ΔT is the temperature rise of the coolant. For a fixed heat load, a smaller temperature rise needs proportionally more flow, and more flow needs more pumping pressure.

  • AOCP lists the CDU selection parameters as cooling capacity, FWS approach temperature, flow rate and pressure, TCS flow and coolant type. It gives an operating range of 17 °C to 65 °C. [5]
  • AOCP's example CDU curves, at 100 L/min of facility flow and 90 L/min of TCS flow, show approach temperature rising with heat load up to 200 kW. The approach temperature is the gap between the two loops. OCP says performance depends on heat-exchanger design and both loops' flows and temperatures. [5] These are example curves for a typical CDU, not a rating for any product.
  • AOCP says facility water should be supplied above the dewpoint to prevent condensation, and that ASHRAE requires coolant to reach IT equipment at least 2 °C above the dewpoint. [5]
  • UThermal resistance from junction to coolant, cold-plate pressure drop and coolant chemistry are not given for any named product in the reviewed sources.

CWhat the choice changes (medium confidence). Air cooling keeps the heat path within the room and its air handlers. Direct-to-chip cooling adds a liquid loop inside the rack, a CDU and a facility water connection, each a separate capability with its own interfaces. That moves part of the thermal dependency from room air handling to plumbing, pumps and water temperature control. A hybrid rack needs both at once.

08

Case study: the GB200 NVL72 rack

This case uses NVIDIA's own documentation: its DGX GB rack user guide (2026-09-17), its DGX GB200 SuperPOD reference architecture (2025-09-03) and its 2024 OCP contribution. [1][2][3] The user guide covers GB200 and GB300 racks together. Vera Rubin NVL72, a later generation, is a different system; its figures are on the interconnect reference and are not mixed with these.

  • AConfiguration. 18 compute trays, each with two Grace CPUs and four Blackwell GPUs, nine NVLink switch trays, two management switches, power shelves, a bus bar and liquid-cooling manifolds. [1]
  • APower. Rack consumption is "approximately 120kW". Power whips from a remote panel feed eight power shelves, each of six air-cooled 5.5 kW PSUs. The shelves convert AC to a nominal 50-51 V DC on a bus bar, with 33 kW input needed per shelf. [1]
  • ABus bar. Rated for 1,400 A, the same width as ORV3 but deeper, which NVIDIA calls a 2x increase in amperage over current standards. [3]
  • ACooling. Liquid runs through rack manifolds and cold plates on the CPUs and GPUs; networking and storage are air-cooled; the liquid system has leak detection. [1] NVIDIA states a cooling capacity of 120 kW is required per rack. Blind-mate manifolds and floating tray connections distribute coolant to compute and switch trays. [3]
  • AFacility. The reference architecture gives each eight-rack scalable unit a 1.2 MW "Thermal Design Power" and recommends Tier 3 or equivalent design. Because of the complex power and cooling, it connects the rack to the building management system. [2]

Where NVIDIA's own figures disagree or do not close

  • URedundancy. The user guide says the eight shelves "provide N+N redundancy". The reference architecture describes each shelf's six PSUs as "configured as N redundancy". [1][2] This page does not choose between them.
  • UBus bar versus load. 1,400 A at 50 V is 70 kW, well below about 120 kW (section 10), so one 1,400 A path cannot carry the whole rack at that voltage. The sources do not state how many paths there are or at what voltage the rating applies.
  • UWhat 120 kW means. NVIDIA does not say whether "approximately 120kW" is a maximum, a typical value or a measurement, or whether it differs between GB200 and GB300.
  • UCoolant. No coolant type, flow rate, supply temperature, pressure drop or ASHRAE liquid-cooling class is stated in the reviewed documents.
09

Facility integration boundaries

A rack's documentation describes what the rack needs. Whether a facility can supply it is a separate question, answered only by evidence about that facility.

  • AFacility water classes. OCP reproduces the ASHRAE TC 9.9 liquid-cooling classes. W17, W27, W32, W40 and W45 have facility water supply temperatures of 17 °C, 27 °C, 32 °C, 40 °C and 45 °C, and W+ is above 45 °C. [5] UASHRAE's own documents were not reviewed, and no reviewed source states which class GB200 NVL72 requires.
  • AWarm-water operation. NVIDIA says liquid cooling lets data centers use warmer water and reduce or eliminate mechanical chillers in many climates. [4] Which heat-rejection equipment a site needs depends on its climate and water temperature, which these sources do not set.
  • AReference designs. Vertiv announced a 7 MW power and cooling reference architecture for GB200 NVL72 supporting up to 132 kW per rack. [6] A reference design states what a design supports; it is not evidence of any built facility.
  • AGrid connection. DOE's advisory board says connection requests for 300 MW to 1,000 MW hyperscale facilities, with lead times of 1 to 3 years, are stretching local grids. [10] A connection request is not energised capacity.
  • ANational energy. DOE reports US data centers used an estimated 176 TWh in 2023 and projects 325 TWh to 580 TWh by 2028. [9] These are modelled estimates and projections, not observed demand at any site.

11

Facility metrics: PUE and WUE

  • SPUE. The Green Grid defines Power Usage Effectiveness as total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. Total facility energy includes power delivery equipment such as switchgear, UPS and batteries, and cooling such as chillers. Only the data-center portion of a facility's utility meter should be measured. [7]
  • SWUE. The Green Grid defines site Water Usage Effectiveness as annual site water use divided by IT equipment energy, in litres per kilowatt-hour, using the same denominator as PUE. A source-based WUE also counts water used to generate the energy. [8]

CBoth metrics divide a facility's energy or water over a period by its IT energy over the same period. Neither can be stated for a server, GPU or rack on its own. A vendor's efficiency multiple is not a PUE or WUE unless it states that boundary and period. ANVIDIA states that GB200 NVL72 delivers "25x more energy efficiency and 300x more water efficiency than traditional air-cooled architectures". [4] UIt gives no baseline, workload or method, so those multiples cannot be checked or converted into facility metrics.

Unresolved questions

These are material questions the reviewed evidence cannot answer. They are recorded, not filled with general industry commentary.

  1. Power-shelf redundancy. N+N (user guide) or N (reference architecture). [1][2]
  2. Bus-bar paths. How a 1,400 A rating at about 50 V carries about 120 kW.
  3. The 120 kW figure. Whether it is a maximum, a typical value or a measurement, and whether it differs between GB200 and GB300.
  4. Component power. Per-GPU, per-superchip and per-tray power for GB200.
  5. Coolant specification. Coolant, flow, supply temperature, pressure drop and W class for GB200 NVL72.
  6. Liquid and air shares of the rack's heat.
  7. ASHRAE primary text. The TC 9.9 guidelines themselves were not reviewed.
  8. Efficiency claims. The method behind NVIDIA's 25x and 300x figures.
  9. Scalable unit. What makes up the 1.2 MW beyond eight racks.
  10. Site evidence. No facility's operating power, cooling or metrics were reviewed.
  11. National estimates. The LBNL and IEA reports themselves could not be retrieved.
