Design, Fabrication, and Packaging

Design, fabrication, and packaging are distinct capabilities with distinct dependencies. In AI accelerators, packaging is no longer the last step: it decides how many dies, and how much memory, can work as one processor.

Treating them as one activity hides where control, access, capacity, and substitution actually reside. The sovereignty stack separates them so evidence can be attached to the correct layer. Sections 04–11 develop the packaging layer into a reference on interposers, silicon bridges, die stacking, chiplet interfaces and the production chain, with AMD Instinct MI300X as a documented case.

Reference summary

Definition. This page covers Ontology Layers 01–03: Design, Fabrication, and Packaging as distinct compute-sovereignty dependencies.

Compute sovereignty relationship. AI chips become deployable only when design rights, manufacturing access, and integration capability align. Each layer carries separate evidence requirements.

Boundary. This page does not verify current capacity, access, or supplier position for any entity. Its packaging sections report what TSMC, AMD and the UCIe Consortium have published, classify each statement, and record where the disclosures stop.

01

Design

Design analysis considers architecture, intellectual property, tools, talent, verification, and the rights needed to turn intent into a manufacturable design.

02

Fabrication

Fabrication analysis considers manufacturing access, process capability, equipment, materials, capacity, yield, and the conditions under which production is available.

03

Packaging

Packaging analysis considers die integration, substrates, assembly, test, thermal design, and the capacity required to turn manufactured dies into deployable systems.

For large AI accelerators, packaging has become a manufacturing capability in its own right. Several dies made on different processes are joined through an interposer or silicon bridges, sometimes stacked on one another first, and placed beside high-bandwidth memory. Each of those steps needs its own equipment and process know-how. The sections below set out what the primary disclosures support about them.

04

Packaging architectures compared

ATSMC groups its advanced packaging as "3DFabric" and splits it in two. The frontend technology, SoIC, stacks silicon on silicon at the wafer level. The backend technologies include CoWoS, in which chips are placed onto pre-made redistribution layers, and SoW, a system-on-wafer service. TSMC states that SoIC-stacked chips "can be subsequently assembled by using conventional packages" or CoWoS and SoW. [1] In other words, 3D stacking and 2.5D interposer integration are separate steps that can be combined.

Within CoWoS, the three variants differ in what carries the wiring between dies. That layer, the interposer, sits between the dies and the package substrate.

CWhat differentiates the variants. On TSMC's descriptions, the CoWoS variants differ chiefly in the interposer: one silicon interposer (S), an RDL interposer (R), or an RDL interposer with embedded silicon bridges (L). Choosing a variant therefore means choosing a different interposer manufacturing route. TSMC names no customer for any variant, so this page does not say why any product uses one.

05

From silicon interposers to silicon bridges

ATSMC states that its advanced-packaging development focus "has shifted from the original Si interposer technology to reconfigured interposers with multiple LSIs". It also says that LSI, with higher routing density and eDTC, "facilitates the expansion of HPC products to larger sizes". [1][2] TSMC measures interposer size in reticles, the largest area a lithography exposure covers, at about 830 mm² each. [2]

CWhat this implies (medium confidence). TSMC reports CoWoS-S at up to 3.3 reticles, and every larger size it reports is CoWoS-L. TSMC also credits LSI with enabling larger sizes. Within TSMC's portfolio, the largest interposer-class packages therefore depend on fabricating LSI bridge chips and embedding them in redistribution layers, not on one larger silicon interposer. The step from 3.3 to 9.5 reticles is close to a threefold increase in interposer area. Our assumption is that the reticle multiples describe interposer area. The sources give no cost, yield or capacity comparison between variants.

06

3D die stacking

  • ATSMC describes SoIC as a "wafer-level frontend 3DIC chip stacking platform" with what it calls outstanding bonding density. It reports N5 SoIC chip-on-wafer stacking in its second year of volume production for HPC in 2024, and 3nm SoIC stacking entering volume production in 2025. [1][2]
  • ATSMC's 2024 report distinguishes SoIC processes by orientation. Face-to-back Gen-1 is in production, and face-to-back Gen-2, "with significant thermal performance improvement", was qualified and started production in 2024. Face-to-face Gen-1 was then "under qualification". [2]
  • UNeither annual report names SoIC's bonding method, such as hybrid bonding, or its bond pitch. TSMC's SoIC technology page could not be retrieved (HTTP 403). This page therefore does not state how SoIC dies are bonded.
  • AThe UCIe 2.0 announcement describes a 3D interface variant, UCIe-3D, as "optimized for hybrid bonding", with bump pitches from 10–25 µm down to 1 µm or less. [4] That is a design target of an interface specification. It is not evidence that SoIC or any named product uses hybrid bonding.

Heat and power in stacked packages

  • ATSMC states that intra-chip heat dissipation "has become a critical issue for 3D integration". At IEDM 2025 it demonstrated aluminium nitride films as a thermal material for 3D ICs. That is a research result, not a production process. [1]
  • ATSMC describes both CoWoS-S and CoWoS-L with an embedded deep trench capacitor (eDTC). [1][2] The reviewed text does not state its electrical role or capacitance. For package-level power and cooling limits, see Power and Thermal Limits.
07

Chiplet interfaces and UCIe

Three different things are often treated as one. An interface standard defines how dies exchange data. An integration architecture defines how dies are physically joined. Demonstrated interoperability means chiplets from different vendors shown to work together, with published test results. Evidence for one does not establish the others.

09

Case study: AMD Instinct MI300X

This case was chosen because AMD's own publications describe its packaging in enough detail to trace each step to a primary source. Other accelerators were not used where no product-level packaging disclosure was found. In particular, no reviewed source names NVIDIA Rubin's packaging technology; see the HBM reference.

  • AAMD's CDNA 3 white paper says MI300 Series products integrate "up to 8 vertically stacked accelerator complex dies (XCD) and 4 I/O dies (IOD)", connected to 8 HBM stacks. The XCDs are fabricated on TSMC's 5nm process. The IODs are made on TSMC's 6nm process and "vertically stacked beneath a pair of XCDs". Each IOD connects through the package to two HBM stacks. [8]
  • AAMD states that MI300X connects 8 accelerator chiplets and 4 IO chiplets "via an interposer using TSMC Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate (CoWoS®) packaging technology". [9] In a post the same day, AMD describes MI300X as using "advanced 3.5D CoWoS packaging", with eight 5nm compute dies layered on top of four 6nm IO dies. [10] CAMD does not define "3.5D" in these posts. We read it as 3D die stacking combined with 2.5D interposer integration, which matches the two statements together.
  • AAMD announced MI300X availability on 2023-12-06. That is an operating product, not a roadmap item. [11]

CWhat the case shows (medium confidence). On AMD's account, one deployable MI300X combines four technology-specific capabilities: two logic processes, a die-stacking step, interposer integration and on-package HBM. AMD attributes only the interposer step to a named packaging technology. The die-stacking method, the CoWoS variant and the HBM suppliers are not disclosed in the reviewed sources. The case therefore establishes that MI300X depends on 3D stacking and on CoWoS-class integration, but not how either step is carried out or by whom. This rests on AMD's statements about its own product; no teardown was reviewed.

11

Unresolved questions

These are material questions the reviewed evidence cannot answer. They are recorded, not filled with general industry commentary.

  1. SoIC bonding method and pitch. TSMC's annual reports describe SoIC's bonding density and status but not its bonding method or pitch. Its SoIC page could not be retrieved.
  2. MI300X die stacking. How XCDs are bonded to IODs, and which company performs the stacking, is not stated in the reviewed AMD sources.
  3. MI300X interposer variant. AMD names TSMC CoWoS and an interposer for MI300X [9], but not the variant or interposer size.
  4. 5.5-reticle CoWoS-L status. TSMC's 2025 report gives two different accounts of its qualification timing. [1]
  5. CoWoS capacity and allocation by variant and customer. No primary source quantifies them, and this project does not estimate them.
  6. UCIe in shipping products. Which products implement UCIe, and whether cross-vendor interoperability has been demonstrated with published compliance results.
  7. UCIe normative text. Only announcements were read. What the UCIe 2.0 and 3.0 specifications require for UCIe-3D and UCIe-A remains unverified.
  8. Test flows. Where interposer-based accelerators are tested (wafer sort, known-good die, after stacking, final package), and by whom.
  9. Substrates. Which organic substrates and suppliers sit under CoWoS-class packages, and whether substrates limit package size.
