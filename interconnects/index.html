Interconnects

The movement of data defines the usable shape of AI compute.

Interconnect analysis examines how data moves within a system, across systems, and through a deployed compute environment. This reference follows data outward from the processor: through on-package fabric and local memory, across links to other accelerators and the host, and into the scale-out network. It shows where each bandwidth figure applies and where two figures cannot be compared.

Evidence compiled: 2026-11-09 · Scientific review pendingVendor and consortium statements remain attributed. Every bandwidth figure is given with its unit, direction and scope. Nothing here establishes measured performance, deployment, interoperability or supply.
  • SStandard text: what a published specification itself states, read directly
  • AAttributed: what a company or consortium disclosed, not independently verified
  • CBounded analysis: our derivation or arithmetic, with stated assumptions
  • UUnresolved: evidence absent, blocked or conflicting
00

Reference summary

Definition. This page covers Ontology Layer 05: Interconnect as the systems that move data within and between AI compute environments.

Compute sovereignty relationship. Interconnect fit affects whether accelerators, memory, and hosts operate as a usable system rather than isolated components.

Boundary. This page does not rank products, providers, or deployments. Its sections report what AMD, NVIDIA, the CXL Consortium and the Ultra Ethernet Consortium have published, classify each statement and record where the disclosures conflict or stop.

01

Within systems

A reference assessment considers fabrics, switching, topology, bandwidth, latency, compatibility, and the dependencies required to connect accelerators, memory, and hosts.

02

Between systems

Scaling across racks, facilities, or regions introduces additional networking, optical, operational, and geographic dependencies that should be mapped separately.

03

No isolated conclusion

Interconnect capability must be interpreted with workload, architecture, deployment, and evidence scope. This page defines the layer; it does not rank products or providers.

The data-movement hierarchy

Data in an accelerator moves through tiers. Each tier is a different physical path with its own bandwidth. In this illustrative selection the reported peak figures decrease outward from compute units, but their measurement domains are not directly comparable. AMD's white paper gives figures for most of these tiers in a single document, which makes the MI300X a usable reference for the shape of the hierarchy. [1]

05

Within the package

AAMD says CDNA 3 uses its Infinity Fabric "more prolifically inside the package". Each XCD's L2 connects to its I/O die over sixteen 64-byte channels, 1 KB per XCD at the interface, and the fabric ties the package together at a stated 4 TB/s. [1] AMD gives no direction or clock for that figure.

UAMD's text gives 34.4 TB/s of aggregate L2 read bandwidth, while a legend in the same paper labels L2-to-XCD bandwidth 51.6 TB/s. The paper does not reconcile the two, so both are recorded as stated. [1]

How dies are physically joined inside a package (interposers, silicon bridges, die stacking) and what the UCIe interface standard covers are set out in the packaging reference. That page also keeps UCIe's announcements separate from product implementation. This page starts where data leaves the compute die.

06

Memory bandwidth versus fabric bandwidth

Five different quantities are often called "bandwidth":

  • HBM interface bandwidth: one stack's interface width × pin rate. Per-stack figures are covered in the HBM reference.
  • Memory-controller capability: how much of that the processor's controllers can drive. UIt is not separately disclosed in the reviewed sources.
  • Aggregate memory bandwidth: all stacks together. AAMD derives MI300X's 5.325 TB/s from an 8,192-bit bus at a 5.2 Gbps data rate. [1]
  • Effective application bandwidth: what a workload achieves. UNo reviewed source measures it.
  • Device-to-device bandwidth: traffic between accelerators over a fabric, a separate physical path with its own figure.

ANVIDIA's 2026-07-21 post states, for one Rubin GPU, up to 22 TB/s of peak HBM4 bandwidth and 3,600 GB/s of NVLink 6 scale-up bandwidth. It also gives 1,800 GB/s of NVLink-C2C bandwidth to the CPU and up to 256 GB/s over x16 PCIe Gen 6. [2] These are four different paths and are not interchangeable figures. NVIDIA's 2026-11-05 post gives 19.2 TB/s for Rubin memory bandwidth instead; that conflict stays unresolved in the HBM reference. [4]

CWhere memory bandwidth ends and fabric bandwidth begins (medium confidence). AMD states that its 896 GB/s P2P figure is bidirectional. If it is also a per-GPU sum, as its arithmetic suggests but AMD does not state, MI300X's 5.3 TB/s peak HBM3 is numerically about 6× that figure; it is about 41× the 128 GB/s single-peer-link figure that AMD's data sheets state. On Rubin, peak HBM4 (22 TB/s, or the later 19.2 TB/s) is about 5× to 6× NVLink 6 (3.6 TB/s bidirectional), which NVIDIA's switches make available toward any peer. [1][2][3][4] These ratios compare stated peaks from different measurement domains. Memory figures are combined read/write capability; link figures count both directions. They show numerical orders of magnitude under stated assumptions, not memory access speed, peer access latency, achieved throughput, or application performance.

Host and CPU interfaces: PCIe, CXL and coherent links

Linking a host to a device is not the same as sharing memory with it. Being able to send data over a link does not make the two sides' memory coherent. A protocol's capability is not a product's configuration. The sources distinguish these layers.

UNVIDIA's own figure for NVLink 6 per Rubin GPU differs across its posts. It is 3.6 TB/s bidirectional on 2026-01-05 and 3,600 GB/s on 2026-07-21, but 3.0 TB/s, with no direction, on 2026-11-05. The later figure was still on the live page on 2026-11-09. [2][3][4] NVIDIA's current product pages split the same way. The Vera Rubin NVL72 page lists 3 TB/s per GPU and 216 TB/s per 72-GPU rack, with no direction stated [13]. The HGX page lists 3.6 TB/s for an individual Rubin GPU, and on the same page 24 TB/s, marked bidirectional, for an eight-GPU NVLink switch: 3 TB/s per GPU if divided evenly, which is our arithmetic [14]. No reviewed NVIDIA document explains the difference, and this page does not choose between the figures.

CWhat the topology changes (medium confidence). In a direct mesh, the link count fixes the domain: seven links reach seven peers, and each pair shares one link. In a switched fabric, the domain is set by switch silicon and rack design, so it can be larger, but it adds a dependency on switch chips. In NVL72, NVIDIA supplies those chips itself. A direct mesh avoids that dependency but caps the domain. This is a structural comparison, not a performance ranking; no measured collective throughput was reviewed for either system.

ANVIDIA gives a rack-level figure for the previous generation, "130 TB/s of aggregate NVLink bandwidth" on GB200 NVL72. [4] An aggregate over 72 GPUs is a system figure and must not be read as one device's bandwidth.

09

From scale-up to scale-out

SThe Ultra Ethernet Specification v1.1 describes three network types: a frontend network, a backend scale-out network and a local scale-up network. Its scale-up examples include "AMD’s XGMI, NVIDIA’s NVLINK, Intel’s Xe Link, switched PCIExpress, and CXL systems". It describes scale-up domains as "limited to dozens, maybe hundreds, of endpoints today". [8] The same text puts endpoint hardware interface details out of scope. It says UE is designed to support the libibverbs and libfabric APIs, and covers PHYs for 100 Gb/s and 200 Gb/s per-lane signalling. [8] These are statements of the specification's own text, from its introductory chapters and contents; most of the 764-page document was not read.

AThe consortium's 1.0 announcement (2025-06-11) says "active implementations and compliance programs" are in progress. It also invites members into "upcoming compliance and interoperability programs". [9] UNo reviewed source names a product that follows the specification or publishes a compliance result.

AFor Vera Rubin, NVIDIA states 1.6 Tb/s of ConnectX-9 scale-out connectivity per GPU, and up to 800 Gb/s of "Ethernet or InfiniBand" connectivity from BlueField-4. It states that Spectrum-6 Ethernet switches use co-packaged optics. Its DGX SuperPOD reference design composes eight NVL72 racks with Spectrum-X Ethernet. [3] No reviewed source states that Spectrum-X follows the Ultra Ethernet specification.

CWhat changes at the rack boundary (medium confidence). Inside NVL72, GPU-to-GPU traffic runs over NVLink switches. Beyond it, traffic runs over NICs, Ethernet switches and optics, on a separate network with its own transport. Per GPU, NVLink 6's 3.6 TB/s (28.8 Tb/s) is about 9× ConnectX-9's 1.6 Tb/s if that figure is per direction, or 18× if it counts both. Scale-out therefore adds NIC, switch, optics and network-software dependencies that the scale-up fabric does not have. The reviewed sources do not show which of those components are interchangeable in practice.

Case study: the AMD MI300X 8-GPU platform

This case was chosen because one primary document, AMD's CDNA 3 white paper with its footnotes, discloses the memory figures, the link counts, the topology and the host connection together. [1] NVIDIA's NVL72 is used above only as a bounded contrast.

QuestionMI300X platformEvidence

2. Which interface carries itInfinity Fabric between GPUs; x16 PCIe Gen 5 to the host CPUA[1]

CChecking AMD's figures (medium confidence). If "32Gbps" is a per-lane rate, an x16 link carries 512 Gb/s, or 64 GB/s, each way: 128 GB/s counting both directions. Seven links give 896 GB/s, AMD's P2P figure. One more x16 link for the host, PCIe Gen 5 at 32 GT/s, adds 128 GB/s, for 1,024 GB/s, AMD's total. AMD's figures are therefore consistent with raw, both-direction sums for one GPU, before encoding overhead. The "8 GPUs" label then names the platform rather than multiplying the figure. AMD's data sheets now state the 128 GB/s bidirectional link figure and "7x 128 GB/s per GPU" directly [10][11], and AMD applies the same "(8 GPUs)" label to MI350's 1,008 GB/s, which its CDNA 4 paper describes as more than 1 TB/s of communication bandwidth per GPU [12]. That makes the per-GPU reading the better-supported one, but no reviewed MI300X document states it, and the platform data sheet's prose describes 896 GB/s as connecting all GPUs in the ring. Editorial review reports that a 2025 edition of AMD's CDNA 4 paper (PID#2258402-C, 10/1/2025) stated the figure per GPU OAM module in endnote MI350-007, which the 2026 edition omits; until that edition is retrieved and checked, the per-GPU reading remains an inference from AMD's other documents.

11

Reading a bandwidth figure

Before comparing two interconnect figures, check six things:

  1. Bits or bytes. Gb/s and Tb/s count bits; GB/s and TB/s count bytes, eight times more.
  2. Transfers or payload. GT/s is a per-lane transfer rate. Payload bandwidth is lower once encoding, flits and error correction are counted.
  3. Direction. A bidirectional figure counts both directions and is twice the per-direction figure.
  4. Scope. Per lane, per link, per device, per switch or per system.
  5. Peak or sustained. Every figure on this page is a stated peak or our arithmetic from one.
  6. Who measured it, and how. No measured figure was reviewed. Latency claims without a stated workload and method are not comparable.

14

Unresolved questions

These are material questions the reviewed evidence cannot answer. They are recorded, not filled with general industry commentary.

  1. NVLink 6 per-GPU figure. 3.6 TB/s bidirectional versus 3.0 TB/s, both from NVIDIA, with its current product pages split the same way (3.6 TB/s on HGX, 3 TB/s on Vera Rubin NVL72). [3][4][13][14]
  2. MI300X link scope. AMD states 128 GB/s bidirectional per link and calls 896 GB/s bidirectional. Whether 896 GB/s is a per-GPU or ring-level figure, and the link encoding, remain unstated. [10][11]
  3. AMD L2 figures. 34.4 TB/s in the text versus 51.6 TB/s in a legend.
  4. Effective bandwidth. No measured collective or application bandwidth for either documented system.
  5. Latency. No measured latency with a stated method.
  6. PCIe primary sources. PCI-SIG documents could not be retrieved.
  7. CXL on accelerators. Whether MI300X or the Rubin GPU implement CXL, and with what behaviour.
  8. CXL normative text. The CXL 3.x and 4.0 specifications were not reviewed.
  9. Ultra Ethernet compliance. No named compliant product or published interoperability result.
  10. Co-packaged optics. No link counts, reach or field reliability for deployed switches.
